🔬 How the analysis works — and where the numbers come from

1. Pose estimation (the only "AI" part). Every sampled video frame is passed through MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (pose_landmarker_full, ~10 MB), a neural network that runs on your own device via WebAssembly + WebGL. It returns 33 named body landmarks per frame — nose, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, heels, toes — as normalized 2-D coordinates plus a visibility confidence. Nothing is uploaded anywhere; the only network request this app ever makes is the one-time download of the model and its WASM runtime from a public CDN.

2. Pixels → metres (calibration). Landmarks are only pixels, so the app converts them to real distances using your height: it measures the pixel span from ankle to shoulder to nose and divides it by the height you entered. That gives a pixels-per-metre scale for the clip. Everything metric (jump height, crouch depth, stance width, hip sag) is a pixel distance divided by this scale — which is why an honest height entry matters more than anything else in the profile.

3. Angles and lines from landmark triples. Joint angles are plain dot-product geometry: e.g. knee angle at the bottom of the dip is the ankle–knee–hip triple, trunk lean is the tilt of the shoulder-midpoint-to-hip-midpoint line off vertical, plank quality is the perpendicular distance of the hip from the shoulder-to-ankle line. No lookup tables, no "form database."

4. Physics from the timeline. The app builds time series (hip height, foot height, elbow angle), median-smooths them, and finds events: crouch = deepest hip before the jump, takeoff/landing = the moments the feet leave and re-cross the estimated floor line (found from your standing ankle height). Jump height comes from flight time using projectile motion, h = g·t²/8 — this is the same flight-time principle a Vertec or contact-mat uses, and it is independent of pixels-per-metre, which makes it the most trustworthy number here. Takeoff velocity is v₀ = g·t/2, and mean power is P = m·g·v₀ using your bodyweight plus the load you entered. Push-up reps are counted by cycling the elbow angle through <110° (bottom) and >150° (top).

5. Sub-frame timing. Takeoff and landing are interpolated between frames rather than snapped to them, so flight time is resolved to roughly a few milliseconds even on a 30 fps clip. Frame rate still matters, though: at 30 fps a 300 ms flight is only ~9 samples, so high-frame-rate (slow-mo) video genuinely improves the read.

6. Scores and feedback. The sub-scores and coaching text are hand-written rule thresholds from standard coaching practice (e.g. 90–110° knee at the bottom of a countermovement, 170–180° for a rigid plank, 30–45° trunk lean during the drive), weighted into the 0–100 composite. The "where this puts you" tiers are published literature / combine-style ranges hardcoded in SPORT_BENCH — e.g. elite volleyball and basketball verticals ~80–90 cm, general population ~25–35 cm. They are population reference ranges, not your personal baseline and not a validated normative database.

5b. Auto-trim (✂). Long clips are mostly standing around, which wastes the pose budget on nothing. The app first scans the whole file at 12 fps, locates the rep — feet-off-floor runs for jumps, elbow minima for push-ups, joint motion energy as a fallback — and then analyzes only that window, plus 1.2 s of standing before it and 0.6 s after (those frames are what the floor line and standing baseline are calibrated from, so they are not dead time). The exact window used is shown in the results and the export, and playback loops just that window. With several attempts in one clip it keeps the highest flight only; with no clear activity contrast it trims nothing rather than guessing.

⚠️ Honest limitations. This is single-camera 2-D estimation, so it cannot see front-to-depth motion: toe-out angle, elbow flare and true bar/hip path are all guesses from one viewpoint, and the near leg reads higher than the far leg (that is why leg symmetry is reported with a caveat). Lens distortion, cropping, motion blur, camera pan/zoom and occlusion by other players all bias the numbers. Flight-time height on a 120 fps clip is coaching-grade and repeatable; force-plate velocity and true joint moments require a lab. Use the trend across sessions, not any single absolute value. No medical advice.

✅ Correct Push-Up Form (checklist)

  1. Hand position: hands slightly wider than shoulders, under (or a few cm below) the shoulder line, fingers spread, middle fingers pointing forward or slightly out.
  2. Body line: one straight line ear → shoulder → hip → ankle. Squeeze glutes, brace the abs like you're about to be punched. No hip sag, no piked hips.
  3. Elbows: tuck ~20–45° toward the body (arrow shape), not flared to a T. Forearms stay vertical.
  4. Depth: lower until elbows pass 90° — chest about a fist's height off the floor. Full range beats half reps every time.
  5. Head/neck: look at the floor ~30 cm ahead of you; spine neutral, don't tuck the chin or crane the neck.
  6. Feet: together or up to hip-width. Wider = easier, narrower = more core demand.
  7. Tempo: ~1.5–2 s down, brief pause, drive up explosively. Don't let gravity do the descent.
  8. Breathing: inhale down, exhale up.

Too hard? Elevate hands on a bench. Too easy? Elevate feet, add a pause at the bottom, or wear a weighted vest.

✅ Correct Jump Squat Form

  1. Stance: feet roughly shoulder-width, toes 5–15° out — the same stance you'd back-squat in.
  2. Descent: hips and knees bend together, 2–3 s controlled down to at least parallel (ideally the depth you'd reach in a competition squat).
  3. Bottom position: chest up, lower back neutral, knees tracking over toes, weight across the whole foot.
  4. Ascent: drive the floor away — explode up as fast as possible, fully extending hips, knees and ankles (triple extension) without leaving the floor… unless jump intent: for a jump squat, take off with feet leaving the floor.
  5. Arms: swing them down and back in the descent, then whip them up past the shoulders at takeoff — arm drive adds 10%+ to jump height.
  6. Head/trunk: eyes forward, head in line with the spine; trunk leans forward 30–45° during the drive — that's normal and powerful.
  7. Load: best power output is usually 0–40% of your 1RM squat (light dumbbells 10–45 lb work great). Too much load kills jump height and slows the training effect.
  8. Landings: land soft on the balls of the feet, absorb through the hips, reset between reps.
